# Dental Injuries? Not on Our Watch! (Deep Dive)


Summer’s all about outdoor play—but it’s also peak season for dental injuries. From pickup games to bike rides, active fun can lead to costly, painful dental trauma. This (deep dive) blog gives comprehensive understanding and tips on protecting your smile, so those sunny memories don’t turn into dental regrets.

By

Oceanic Dental

July 31, 2025


## Summer Fun, Dental Bummer?


The irresistible call of summer—sunshine, outdoor adventures, and the thrill of sports—is a powerful one. Whether it involves a friendly game of pickleball, a spirited softball match, or just a bike ride through the park, summer activities are fantastic for health and happiness. Yet, amidst all the fun, there lurks a sneaky little spoiler: dental injuries. These incidents are far more common than many might realize, especially when the weather warms up and everyone heads outside. As Krystal Pham, D.D.S., lead dentist at Oceanic Dental, notes, "With longer days come brighter moments outdoors—but every dive, dash, and swing can put your smile at risk..".

The prevalence of sports-related dental injuries is striking. Approximately 15 million individuals in the United States experience some type of sports-related dental injury each year, with around 10 percent of players sustaining a dental or facial injury in any given athletic season.2 Studies indicate that about 32 percent of all facial and dental traumas are linked to sports.3 For children under 18, an average of 22,000 dental injuries occurred annually between 1990 and 2003.4 The risk significantly escalates for those without proper protection; athletes are reported to be 60 times more likely to suffer damage to their teeth if they are not wearing a mouthguard.2

The consequences of dental injuries extend far beyond a momentary "ouch!" They can lead to significant pain, require costly and extensive repairs, result in long-term oral health issues, affect overall well-being, and even sideline athletes from their favorite activities.6 For instance, the lifetime cost of an avulsed, or knocked-out, tooth in a teenage athlete can reach a staggering $20,000, frequently exceeding the maximum benefits provided by most insurance companies.4 This financial burden underscores that preventing such injuries is not merely a health concern but also a prudent financial decision. This article serves as a comprehensive guide for keeping those pearly whites safe, ensuring summer memories are filled with smiles, not dental woes.


## The Uninvited Guests: Common Summer Sports Dental Injuries


When an unexpected collision or fall occurs during summer activities, teeth can suffer a variety of injuries. These are not minor inconveniences; they can be painful, necessitate extensive treatment, and have lasting consequences.

The most common dental injuries observed include:

Crown Fractures (Chipped or Cracked Teeth): These injuries occur when a piece of the tooth breaks off, often as a result of an object hitting the face.1 Symptoms can range from increased cold sensitivity to sharp pain when biting down, or pain that appears intermittently.1 Cracks can vary in severity, from superficial "craze lines" in the enamel to severe fractures that extend below the gumline, potentially exposing the tooth's sensitive pulp and requiring complex treatments like root canals or even extraction.2 Crown fractures are the most frequently observed injury to permanent teeth.4

Dental Avulsions ("Knocked-Out" Teeth): This occurs when an entire tooth is completely dislodged from its socket.1 It constitutes a dental emergency with a critical, narrow time window for successful replantation.1

Horizontal Root Fractures: In this type of injury, the root of the tooth fractures below the bone level. It may also be associated with the tooth being displaced from its original position.1 An X-ray scan is typically required for accurate diagnosis.1

Displaced Teeth: The tooth remains within its socket but has been moved out of its normal alignment.1

Soft Tissue Injuries: Lacerations to the lips, gums, tongue, or cheeks are also frequently encountered.4 While mouthguards primarily offer protection to the teeth, they may not fully prevent these soft tissue injuries.4

The location and mechanism of these injuries often vary by sport. The majority of sports-related dental injuries affect the upper lip, the maxilla (upper jaw), and particularly the maxillary incisors (front upper teeth), with 50 to 90 percent of dental injuries involving these prominent front teeth.4

Certain sports and activities carry a higher risk for dental trauma:

Baseball: This sport accounts for the highest number of dental injuries in the 7-to-12-year-old age group.3

Basketball: It is the most frequent sport associated with dental injuries in the 13-to-17-year age group.3 High rates are also observed in boys' basketball at the high school level.4

Girls' Field Hockey: This sport shows some of the highest rates of dental injuries among high school athletes.4

Other popular sports with significant risk include Soccer, Softball, Wrestling, Volleyball, and Gymnastics.4 Even seemingly low-risk activities such as cycling or trampolines can lead to oral injuries if an accident occurs.4

The nature of contact also differs: player-player contact is common in basketball, while player-object contact (e.g., with a ball or bat) is more prevalent in baseball, softball, and field hockey.4 This distinction is important because it suggests that while a mouthguard is universally beneficial, the specific type of protective gear might need to be considered. For example, sports with high player-object impact might warrant additional facial protection, such as fielding masks for younger baseball players 9, beyond just a mouthguard. In contrast, in player-player contact sports, the mouthguard's ability to cushion blows and prevent jaw fractures is paramount.9 This deeper understanding of "appropriate protection" guides equipment choices.

Furthermore, pre-existing conditions can increase vulnerability. Children with significant overjet, commonly known as "buck teeth," have a greater chance of experiencing dental trauma.3 This highlights a crucial intrinsic risk factor that can be identified and potentially mitigated before an injury occurs. This implies that regular dental check-ups are not solely for cavity prevention but also for identifying and addressing anatomical vulnerabilities that heighten sports injury risk, possibly through early orthodontic intervention.3 This connects preventive dentistry directly to sports safety in a proactive manner.


## Your Smile's Super Shield: The Mighty Mouthguard


Mouthguards are the unsung heroes of sports safety. These protective devices provide a crucial cushion that absorbs and distributes the force of impact, significantly reducing the risk of broken teeth, injuries to the lips and tongue, and even jaw fractures.5 Beyond physical protection, wearing a mouthguard can also boost an athlete's confidence, allowing them to play more aggressively and without fear of injury.5 Dentistry's greatest contribution to the sports world is encouraging the consistent use of mouthguards at all ages when participating in competitive activities.1

The effectiveness of a mouthguard, however, varies significantly depending on its type. Not all mouthguards are created equal.13 Understanding the differences is key to choosing the best protection for one's smile.


### Mouthguard Showdown: Choosing Your Smile's Best Protector


The common belief that boil-and-bite mouthguards offer sufficient protection is a significant public health concern. Evidence clearly indicates that these mouthguards can decrease in occlusal thickness by 70-99% during the molding process, thereby diminishing their protective properties.14 Sports dentists explicitly do not recommend store-bought boil-and-bite mouthguards for adequate protection.14 This suggests that many athletes relying on these popular, accessible options may have a false sense of security, potentially leading to injuries they believed they were protected from. This highlights a critical educational gap that requires attention.

Mouthguards offer dual protection: not only do they safeguard teeth, but they also protect the brain by absorbing some of the force of an impact, potentially helping to protect against jaw fractures and concussions from traumatic blows to the chin.9 While the National Athletic Trainers' Association states that mouthguards are not definitively proven to prevent concussions 15, their ability to absorb impact forces can contribute to overall impact management. This adds a significant layer of value to mouthguard use beyond just dental safety, appealing to athletes and parents concerned about head injuries.

The effectiveness of a mouthguard is not solely dependent on its physical characteristics; it also relies heavily on athlete compliance, proper design, fit, and consistent maintenance.15 A perfectly designed custom mouthguard offers no benefit if it is not worn consistently or if it is poorly maintained. This emphasizes that education must extend beyond merely knowing what to purchase to understanding how to properly use and care for the protective gear, fostering a culture where wearing such equipment is normalized and encouraged.3


### Choosing a Champion: What to Look for in a Mouthguard


Fit is Paramount: A custom-made mouthguard, precisely crafted by a dentist from an impression of the individual's teeth, is unequivocally the best choice.5 It ensures precise adaptation, retention, and comfort, guaranteeing it remains securely in place and provides maximum protection.

Thickness Matters (But Not Excessively): Dr. Keith Hunter, an Australian sports dentist, suggests specific thicknesses for optimal protection without excessive bulk: 3mm for the labial (front) and occlusal (biting surface) areas, and 2mm for the palatal (roof of mouth) area.14 Custom guards can be multi-layered to achieve the necessary thickness for full-contact sports.14

Material Quality: The material used should be biocompatible and possess excellent physical properties, typically a poly (ethylene vinyl acetate-EVA) copolymer.14

Full Coverage: An effective mouthguard should cover all posterior teeth and allow for proper tooth and gum adaptation without impinging on muscle attachments.14 Studies have shown that many boil-and-bite mouthguards fail to cover all posterior teeth.14

Special Considerations: For individuals wearing braces, specialized mouthguards are designed to fit over them, providing the same level of protection.5 For athletes in a growth phase, mouthguards should be replaced at the beginning of every sport season to avoid affecting tooth development.5


### Keeping Your Super Shield Fresh: Mouthguard Care & Replacement Tips


Cleanliness is Key: The mouthguard should be brushed daily.9 Periodically, it can be soaked in an over-the-counter denture cleaner.9

Storage Savvy: Softer mouthguards may benefit from being stored in water to prevent dehydration.9 It should always be stored in a sturdy, ventilated container when not in use.

When to Replace: Mouthguards are generally designed to last for at least two years.14 However, for growing athletes or those with braces, replacement at the beginning of each sports season is recommended.5 It should be replaced immediately if it shows any signs of wear, tears, or a poor fit.


## Beyond the Guard: Other Smile-Saving Strategies


Protecting one's smile during sports extends beyond simply wearing a mouthguard. It involves a comprehensive approach to oral health that builds resilience and minimizes risk.


### The Power of Prevention: Your Dental Pit Crew!


Regular Dental Check-ups and Cleanings: These routine visits are crucial for maintaining oral health. Keeping teeth healthy is the first line of defense against them becoming damaged.16 Regular cleanings, typically twice a year, help prevent oral infections by removing bacteria and plaque, which can weaken teeth and make them more susceptible to injury.16 Dentists can identify and treat cavities early, preventing more significant problems.16

Identifying Vulnerabilities: During check-ups, dentists can also assess anatomical factors such as significant overjet, which can increase the likelihood of dental trauma.3 Early orthodontic care might be recommended to address these issues and mitigate the risk of injury.3

Oral Health and Performance: A healthy mouth is intricately linked to an athlete's overall physical condition and performance.6 Oral problems like tooth decay and gum disease can cause inflammation or infections throughout the body, potentially impacting well-being and athletic performance.7 A dental emergency occurring just before a competition can impair performance or even prevent participation entirely.7 This connection highlights that dental care is not merely cosmetic or reactive but a proactive component of sports medicine and achieving peak performance.

Amateur Athletes and Risk: Less-professional athletes exhibit a higher prevalence of sports-related injuries, and amateur athletes are more prone to experiencing such injuries than elite athletes.4 Elite athletes often benefit from extensive support, including top-tier coaching, medical staff, and mandated protective gear. Amateur athletes, however, may lack awareness, proper training, or access to high-quality equipment. This suggests that public health campaigns and educational efforts should specifically target amateur leagues, school sports, and recreational participants, as they represent a significant and vulnerable population.


### Fueling a Strong Smile: Diet and Hydration


Smart Snacking: A healthy diet contributes to strong teeth. Incorporating green leafy vegetables, crunchy fruits and vegetables, nuts, dairy products, and lean protein can help re-mineralize teeth, making them stronger and more resistant to injury.16

Beware of Sports Drinks: While popular, many sports beverages, gels, and carbohydrate mouth rinses contain high levels of free sugars and acidity. Frequent consumption can damage dental hard tissues and dental materials.7 Opting for water as the primary hydration source is a healthier alternative.

Hydration Heroes: Drinking plenty of water helps the body produce saliva, which naturally washes away bacteria and food particles, contributing to overall oral health.16


### Other Protective Gear: More Than Just Mouthguards!


Helmets with Faceguards: For sports like football or hockey, a sturdy helmet equipped with a faceguard provides an essential additional layer of protection for the face and jaw.2

Fielding Masks: For children up to 12 years of age, particularly in high-velocity ball or puck sports such as baseball, softball, lacrosse, and hockey, the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry recommends a fielding mask in addition to, or sometimes instead of, a mouthguard.9 These masks can prevent very serious injuries for children who possess the strength to hit a ball hard but may not yet have the reflexes to avoid impact.9


### Parental and Coach Power: Cultivating a Culture of Safety


Lead by Example: The attitudes of parents and coaches toward mouthguards and other protective gear significantly influence their adoption.3 Promoting protective gear and making it a non-negotiable part of sports participation is vital.

Educate Young Athletes: It is important to explain to children why dental safety is crucial, using relatable analogies such as a "Superhero Mouthguard" that protects their smile.17 Helping them understand that, just like helmets and pads protect their heads and bodies, a mouthguard shields their teeth, can foster acceptance and consistent use.17

Early Adoption: The earlier athletes begin practicing with new protective equipment, the better.3

Establishing a "Dental Home": Proactively establishing a "dental home"—a dentist one regularly visits—is highly advantageous. This involves discussing emergency protocols with the dentist in advance.9 In a high-stress situation, having an established relationship with a familiar dentist means the individual (and their family) will be treated by someone they know, and the practice's emergency procedures (during and after hours) will already be clear. This significantly reduces anxiety and improves the chances of a positive outcome by streamlining the response process, transforming a reactive crisis into a more manageable situation.9


## Oops! A Dental Mishap? Your Game Plan for Emergencies


Accidents can happen, even with the most diligent preventive measures. The key to a successful outcome is remaining calm and acting swiftly. The first 30 to 60 minutes after a dental injury are crucial for determining if a tooth can be repaired or saved.9 For a knocked-out tooth, the window for successful replantation is even tighter—ideally within 5 minutes for the best chance of success.15 This critical "golden window" for avulsed teeth is often far shorter than many realize, emphasizing the need for immediate action. The difference between 5 minutes and 30 minutes is substantial in a real-life emergency. This highlights that education must stress immediate action and preparedness (e.g., knowing how to replant, having milk or a tooth preservation kit ready) rather than just "getting to a dentist soon."

Having an established "dental home"—a dentist one regularly visits—is invaluable. Knowing whom to call and their emergency protocols can make a stressful situation much easier to manage.9


### Emergency Kit Essentials: Be Prepared, Not Scared!


Keeping a small dental emergency kit readily accessible in a gym bag or car can make a significant difference in an urgent situation.9

What to include:

A small container with a lid (for storing a knocked-out tooth).12

Milk or saline solution (for tooth storage).9

Clean gauze or a handkerchief.12

Over-the-counter pain relief (ibuprofen or acetaminophen).9

Dental wax or sugar-free chewing gum (for covering sharp edges).12

The dentist's emergency contact information!


## Keeping the Sparkle: Post-Injury Care & Long-Term Health



### What to Expect After an Injury: Healing and Follow-up


After a dental injury, the treating dentist will provide specific instructions for care. This often includes consuming only soft foods and liquids for a period, typically two weeks, avoiding extremely hot or cold foods, gentle brushing with a soft toothbrush, and rinsing with an antibacterial mouthwash.10 Regular follow-up appointments are essential to monitor the healing process and check for complications such as continued pain, swelling, bleeding, or tooth discoloration.10


### The Long Game: Maintaining Oral Health for a Lifetime of Smiles


Sports dentistry plays a vital role in the long-term health of an athlete's smile by minimizing risks and effectively addressing trauma.6 Repeated dental trauma can lead to chronic pain, temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorders, and even tooth loss.6 This is a significant consideration, as TMJ disorders can cause widespread pain, difficulty chewing, and headaches, impacting an individual's quality of life long after the initial injury. This highlights that comprehensive sports dentistry must consider not just immediate repair but also the prevention of cumulative trauma and its systemic effects on the masticatory system. By prioritizing dental safety now, individuals can avoid these complications and maintain optimal oral health throughout their lives.6

Continued good oral hygiene practices, including brushing with fluoride toothpaste and regular flossing, remain fundamental.16 It is important to remember that prevention is not only the best option for health but also a financially sound decision.9 Innovations in sports dentistry are continuously enhancing safety. The field continues to evolve, with advancements in materials and technology improving the effectiveness of protective devices.6 Innovations such as 3D-printed mouthguards and impact sensors are pushing the boundaries of what is possible in dental safety, indicating that protective gear is not static and new technologies are emerging that offer even better fit, comfort, and potentially real-time data on impact, further improving safety.6


## Share the Smile: A Call to Action


Equipped with the knowledge to protect teeth from summer sports injuries, individuals can become champions for oral health. This valuable information should be shared with friends, family, teammates, and coaches. Encouraging everyone to invest in a quality mouthguard, prioritize regular dental check-ups, and understand essential first-aid steps can make a significant difference. By spreading awareness and promoting preventive measures, everyone can contribute to a safer, healthier, and happier summer for every smile.

Enjoy summer adventures, worry-free and with a dazzling, protected smile!

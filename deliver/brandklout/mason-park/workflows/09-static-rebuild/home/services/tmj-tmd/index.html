Is this you

Eight symptoms, and only three of them are jaw pain.

TMD symptoms mimic other conditions, which is why patients so often arrive having been treated for migraine or an ear infection first.

01

Jaw pain or tenderness

Persistent, and worse when speaking or chewing.

02

Headaches around the temples

Frequent headaches or migraines, sited exactly where the jaw muscle is.

03

Clicking or popping

Or a grating sound, when you open or close your mouth.

04

Ear symptoms

Earache, ringing (tinnitus), or a sense of fullness in the ear with nothing wrong in it.

And the four that people least expect to be a jaw problem

  • Restricted openingDifficulty fully opening or closing your mouth.
  • LockingThe jaw locking, either open or closed. Alarming, and a clear sign.
  • Facial swellingAlong the side of the face.
  • Neck and shoulder painAssociated with jaw tension, and treated for years as a posture problem.

The Treatment

What it is, and why it happens.

What are the TMJ and TMD?

The temporomandibular joints are the two small joints either side of your face, connecting jaw to skull. TMD is what happens when they become misaligned, inflamed or simply overworked.

In the practice’s words

The temporomandibular joints (TMJ) are the small, delicate joints located on each side of your face, connecting your jawbone to your skull. They allow you to move your jaw up and down and side to side, making it possible to talk, chew, and yawn. When these joints become misaligned, inflamed, or overworked, it can lead to TMD — a painful condition affecting jaw function and comfort.

Our goal is to relieve discomfort, restore proper jaw function, and prevent long-term complications. Every patient receives a customized TMD treatment plan designed specifically to address their unique symptoms and lifestyle.

Six causes, and the treatment follows the cause rather than the symptom

  • InjuryA blow to the face, or direct trauma to the jaw.
  • GrindingChronic bruxism, usually during sleep. The most common cause, and the most treatable — see night guards.
  • ArthritisOr other degenerative joint disease affecting the jaw.
  • BiteMisaligned teeth or abnormal bite patterns, which put the joint under constant uneven load.
  • StressAnxiety leading to jaw clenching, awake as well as asleep.
  • PosturePoor posture affecting jaw alignment. The one nobody connects.

The Options

Most cases never need surgery.

The practice’s own five non-invasive therapies. These are what the great majority of patients are treated with.

01

A custom night guard

Prevents nighttime grinding, easing the pressure on the joint. Often the whole treatment. See night guards.

02

Oral splints and bite appliances

Worn day or night to stabilise the jaw and correct its positioning.

03

Physical therapy

Exercises and stretches to improve jaw mobility, flexibility and strength.

04

Stress management

Counselling or relaxation techniques, because unconscious clenching has a cause worth addressing.

05

Medication

Anti-inflammatories or muscle relaxants, prescribed to manage the symptoms while the cause is treated.

Rarely surgeryExtreme cases only

Where non-surgical methods are not enough: corticosteroid injections, orthodontic treatment to correct the bite, or — in extreme cases only — arthroscopy or open joint surgery.

The Path

What happens, step by step.

01

History

A comprehensive review of your dental and medical history. Much of the answer is usually in here.

02

Physical examination

Jaw movement and muscle tenderness, examined by hand. Where it clicks, where it catches, where it hurts.

03

Imaging

Digital x‑rays or 3D imaging, to assess the joint and the bone structure around it.

04

Bite analysis

How the teeth meet, and whether that is loading the joint unevenly.

05

Habits, discussed

Clenching, grinding, stress, posture. This conversation often changes the plan more than the imaging does.

How it compares.

The teeth stop wearing down. Grinding continues, and so does the damage.
Joint degeneration is prevented. The joint keeps taking the load.
Sleep quality improves. Night-time clenching is untouched.
Usually an appliance, not surgery. Indefinite, with no endpoint.

Anti-inflammatories and muscle relaxants have a real place here — they are one of the five therapies. The distinction is using them alongside treatment rather than instead of it.

Why a dentist, for a joint problem

Because the commonest causes are dental ones, and the commonest treatment is a dental appliance.

  • GrindingDiagnosed from the wear pattern on your teeth, which only a dentist looks at.
  • The biteBite analysis is a dental examination, and a misaligned bite is a dental cause.
  • The applianceA night guard or splint is made from impressions of your own teeth.
  • The damageCracked and flattened teeth from clenching are repaired here too — a crown, or a filling.
  • Whole-patient focusThe practice addresses the emotional and lifestyle factors alongside the physical ones.

Questions

Questions patients ask before they decide.

Will I need surgery?

Almost certainly not. Most cases of TMD are treated with non-invasive methods: a custom night guard, an oral splint or bite appliance, physical therapy, stress management, or medication. Corticosteroid injections, orthodontic treatment and joint surgery are reserved for cases where those are insufficient, and surgery only in extreme ones.

Why would a dentist treat my headaches?

Because the commonest cause of TMD is grinding, which is diagnosed from the wear pattern on your teeth, and the commonest treatment is an appliance made from impressions of them. Headaches around the temples are one of the eight recognised TMD symptoms.

My jaw clicks but does not hurt. Should I come in?

Worth mentioning at your next checkup. Clicking, popping or grating when opening or closing is one of the symptoms, and catching it before there is pain means preventing long-term joint degeneration rather than treating it.

Could my ear problem actually be my jaw?

It can be. Earache, ringing in the ears and a sense of ear fullness are all recognised TMD symptoms, and they mimic other conditions closely enough that professional diagnosis is essential.

What causes it?

Six things: jaw injury or facial trauma, chronic teeth grinding, arthritis or degenerative joint disease, misaligned teeth or an abnormal bite, stress and anxiety leading to clenching, and poor posture affecting jaw alignment. Identifying which is yours is what the diagnosis is for.

How is it diagnosed?

A comprehensive review of your dental and medical history, a physical examination of jaw movement and muscle tenderness, digital x‑rays or 3D imaging of the joint and bone, a bite alignment analysis, and a discussion of habits like clenching and grinding.

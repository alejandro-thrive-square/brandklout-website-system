What We Teach

What it is, and what it covers.

What is hygiene education?

Sitting with a hygienist and going through what you actually do at the sink, then fixing the parts that are not working. It is the least glamorous service here and the one with the longest reach.

In the practice’s words

Dental hygiene education teaches patients the best practices for maintaining strong teeth, healthy gums, and fresh breath. It includes everything from basic brushing techniques to understanding which foods are good (or bad) for your smile.

At Mason Park Dental, preventive dental care is woven into everything we do. Our education-focused approach ensures that you’re not just a passive patient, but an active participant in your oral wellness journey. Dr. Dave Cho simplifies complex dental concepts into actionable advice — his goal is that every patient walks out empowered, with not just a cleaner mouth but a better understanding of how to keep it that way.

The six things the programme covers

  • BrushingEffectively, and safely. Those are two separate problems and most people have one of them.
  • FlossingThe correct way — and yes, there is a right way, which is why the practice says so.
  • Your toothbrush and toothpasteChoosing the right ones for your enamel, your gums and your habits.
  • Mouthwash and rinsesWhich ones do something, and when in the routine to use them.
  • FluorideWhat it actually does to enamel, so the advice makes sense rather than being a rule.
  • Gum diseaseRecognising the early signs yourself, between visits.

The Technique

Five corrections that do most of the work.

The practice’s own list. Simple changes, and the difference between them and what most people do is substantial.

01

45 degrees, not flat

Hold the brush at a 45-degree angle to the gumline. This one change reaches where decay starts.

02

Small circles, not scrubbing

Circular motions rather than side to side. Sawing back and forth wears the enamel at the gumline.

03

Soft bristles

Firm brushes do not clean better. They remove enamel and push gums back.

04

Floss against the sides

Not just down between the teeth. The plaque is on the tooth faces, and that is what the floss has to touch.

05

A fluoride rinse

Added at the end, for the protection brushing alone does not give.

◆

And two minutes

The length the practice teaches children, and the one most adults have quietly stopped doing.

Diet

What to lean on, and what to limit.

The practice’s own two lists.

Lean on vs Limit
Leafy greens. Sugary snacks and soda.
Dairy products. Acidic citrus fruit, in quantity.
Crunchy fruit and vegetables. Sticky candy that clings to teeth.
Water, throughout the day. Anything sipped slowly over hours.

The practice is explicit that these are not about restriction but about balance. Moderation is the whole instruction on the citrus.

Gum disease: the four signs to know

Nearly half of adults over 30 have some form of it. Caught as gingivitis it reverses; left, it does not.

  • Red, swollen or bleeding gumsBleeding when you brush is not normal, and is the earliest sign.
  • Persistent bad breathThe kind brushing does not fix.
  • Receding gum linesTeeth appearing longer than they used to.
  • Sensitivity or loosenessA tooth that has moved has lost support around it.

Any of these means booking, not waiting. See deep cleaning for what happens if it has already progressed.

Questions

Questions patients ask before they decide.

Is there really a wrong way to floss?

Yes. Most people push the floss straight down between the teeth and pull it out again. The plaque is on the sides of the teeth, so the floss has to curve against each tooth face in turn. It is the single most common correction we make.

How long should I brush?

Two minutes, twice a day, at a 45-degree angle to the gumline, in small circular motions, with a soft-bristled brush.

Should I use a harder brush to clean better?

No. Soft bristles protect enamel. A firm brush combined with a side-to-side scrubbing motion is one of the commonest causes of gum recession and sensitivity we see.

Do I still need a professional cleaning if my routine is good?

Yes. Even with perfect habits, plaque builds up where a brush cannot reach, and hardened tartar cannot be removed at home at all. See cleanings and exams.

What are the early signs of gum disease?

Red, swollen or bleeding gums; persistent bad breath; receding gum lines; and tooth sensitivity or looseness. Nearly half of adults over 30 are affected, and caught early as gingivitis it is reversible.

Is this only for children?

No. Children get age-appropriate lessons, but the adults who benefit most are the ones with frequent cavities, sensitive teeth, braces or aligners, or a long gap since their last visit.

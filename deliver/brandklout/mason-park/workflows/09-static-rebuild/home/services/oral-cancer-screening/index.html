Is this you

Six signs worth a phone call.

Most of these turn out to be nothing. That is not a reason to leave one of them for a few more months.

01

A sore that will not heal

Persistent, rather than the ulcer that comes and goes.

02

White or red patches

Anywhere inside the mouth, including under the tongue.

03

Chewing or swallowing is hard

A change in how food moves, not a one-off.

04

A lump or thickening

In the cheek, the neck, or under the jaw.

05

A chronic sore throat, or hoarseness

A voice change that has outlasted a cold.

06

Numbness

In the mouth or the face, with no dental work to explain it.

And who the practice recommends an annual screening for

  • Tobacco usersCigarettes, cigars, pipes, chewing tobacco or vaping. Long-term users should be screened more often.
  • Regular drinkersAlcohol irritates the mucous membranes. Combined with tobacco, the risk rises exponentially.
  • HPV exposureHPV-16 contributes to oropharyngeal cancers and often goes undetected for years, in people with no other risk factors at all.
  • A family history of cancerOf any kind, not only oral.
  • And othersFrequent sun exposure (linked to lip cancer), being over 40, poor nutrition or a compromised immune system, and a history of cancer elsewhere in the body.

The Screening

What it is, and why it is standard here.

Why it is part of every checkup

Oral cancer is one of the most underdiagnosed cancers, largely because it produces no noticeable symptoms until it has progressed. The practice does not wait for symptoms — the screening is built into the routine visit.

In the practice’s words

When oral cancer is caught early, it’s much more manageable. The treatment is less invasive, the recovery is quicker, and the survival rate is significantly higher. According to the Oral Cancer Foundation, the five-year survival rate for oral cancer jumps to nearly 80–90% when detected early, compared to just 30–40% in late-stage diagnoses.

Early detection gives patients a fighting chance before cancer spreads to surrounding tissues or other parts of the body. It allows us to take swift, targeted action, which could mean the difference between a simple outpatient procedure and more aggressive treatments like surgery or radiation.

We use advanced screening technology to enhance accuracy, and we document each exam to monitor any changes over time. If a suspicious area is identified, we take the appropriate steps for further testing or professional referrals.

Seven places it can appear, and most of them are out of your own sight

  • The lipsAnd the corners of the mouth. Sun exposure is the risk factor here.
  • The cheeksThe inner lining, which you never look at.
  • The gums and jawlineWhere a thickening reads as ordinary soreness.
  • The tongueThe front, the sides, and the floor of the mouth beneath it.
  • The palateBoth the soft and the hard palate.
  • The throatAnd the tonsillar area, where a lump is easy to dismiss.
  • The lymph nodesFelt for by hand, under the jaw and down the neck.

  • 3Advanced tools, where needed
The advanced tools, and when they are used
  • VELscope® and similar light-based devices. Fluorescence technology that reveals changes in tissue structure not visible to the naked eye.
  • Tissue staining. Toluidine Blue dye adheres to areas of higher cellular activity, flagging abnormal areas for a closer look.
  • Brush biopsies. Cells collected for laboratory evaluation, if something suspicious is found.

These are used when the visual and physical examination raises a question, not routinely. The screening itself is the light, the mirror and the hands.

Oral cancer doesn’t exist in a vacuum — studies show links between oral and systemic health, and a delayed diagnosis can affect your immune system, respiratory function, and overall quality of life.

The Path

What happens, step by step.

01

Visual examination

An overhead light and a mirror, over the lips, cheeks, tongue, palate, floor of the mouth and throat. Looking for white or red patches, sores, swelling and discolouration.

02

Physical palpation

Gently feeling the floor of the mouth, under the tongue, the neck, the jawline and the lymph nodes, for lumps, nodules or thickening.

03

Advanced tools, if needed

Fluorescence imaging, tissue staining, or a brush biopsy, only where the first two steps raise a question.

04

What happens if something is found

Further testing, or a referral to the right specialist, promptly. You are told what was seen and what it might be.

◆

Do not wait for symptoms.

By the time oral cancer produces symptoms it has usually progressed. That is the entire argument for screening while nothing is wrong.

Questions

Questions patients ask before they decide.

How often should I be screened?

At least once a year, during a dental checkup. For higher-risk patients, twice a year may be advised. Dr. Sung will discuss the right timeline based on your personal risk factors and medical history.

Does it hurt?

No. The screening is completely non-invasive and takes just a few minutes. There are no needles, no pain and no downtime, and it is integrated into your regular checkup.

I do not smoke or drink. Do I still need it?

Yes. HPV-16 contributes to oropharyngeal cancers and often goes undetected for years, and people with none of the traditional risk factors can still be affected. Every adult benefits from regular screening regardless of age or current health.

What happens if you find something?

If a suspicious area is identified, we take the appropriate steps for further testing or a professional referral. Enhanced tools — fluorescence imaging, tissue staining, or a brush biopsy for laboratory evaluation — may be used first to get a clearer picture.

Where does oral cancer usually appear?

The lips, the inside of the cheeks, the gums and jawline, the tongue and the floor of the mouth, the soft and hard palate, and the throat and tonsillar area. Most of those are hidden or easy to overlook, which is why signs go unnoticed without a professional evaluation.

Why does a dentist do this rather than a doctor?

Because a dentist looks inside your mouth twice a year and has a record of what it looked like last time. The screening is documented at each exam so changes over time are visible.

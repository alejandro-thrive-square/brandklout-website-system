Some faces have not lost volume. They were built with less projection through the cheekbone or the chin, and no amount of filler placed in the soft tissue above will substitute for the platform that is missing underneath.

An implant addresses the skeleton directly. It is a permanent, precisely shaped augmentation that sits on the bone, and because it is a solid object in a fixed position it does not migrate, dissolve or need topping up.

What this treats

Who it suits

The clearest candidates are people who have been offered filler repeatedly and have never been satisfied with it, because the deficiency they are trying to correct is structural.

A recessed chin is worth singling out. It is one of the most under-diagnosed features in facial aesthetics, and correcting it changes the apparent size of the nose and the definition of the jawline without touching either.

  • 01

    A flat or hollow midface where the cheekbone gives no definition in profile

  • 02

    A chin that sits back from the rest of the face, which makes the nose read as larger and the neck as shorter

  • 03

    Facial balance that photographs poorly in profile despite everything looking reasonable head on

  • 04

    Volume loss that filler improves for a few months at a time and never actually solves

Often combined with

Implants set the frame. What sits on top of it is frequently addressed in the same plan:

  • Jawline implant, where projection is needed along the whole mandibular border rather than at the chin alone.
  • Buccal fat pad reduction, for definition through the mid-face once the cheekbone has a platform.
  • Facial liposuction under the chin, where a stronger chin reveals fullness that was previously camouflaged.

Planned to your bone, and reversible

Implants are sized and shaped against your own skeletal measurements rather than selected from a small range of stock sizes and hoped for. That planning is the difference between an implant that reads as your own bone and one that reads as an implant.

They are also removable. That is a genuine and underrated advantage over anything permanent that cannot be undone: if it is ever not right, it comes out, and the anatomy returns to where it started.

Dr. Stone trained in oral and maxillofacial surgery across three Level 1 trauma centres. Placing hardware on facial bone through the mouth is the core of that specialty rather than an addition to it.

The procedure

What the day actually looks like.

01

In theatre

One to two hours, under sedation or general anaesthetic depending on what is combined. Access is through the mouth for both cheek and chin in most cases, so there is no external incision. Same day home.

02

The first fortnight

Swelling is the main feature and it is significant for the first week. A soft diet and careful oral hygiene for that period. Numbness of the lower lip after a chin implant is common early on and resolves.

03

Settling

The shape you will keep emerges as swelling resolves over six to eight weeks. The implant itself does not change after that.

Questions

What people ask before they book.

Can it be removed if I change my mind?

Yes, and that is one of the strongest arguments for an implant over the alternatives. It is a discrete object in a defined pocket. Removal is a smaller procedure than placement and the anatomy returns to its starting point.

Why not just use filler?

Filler sits in soft tissue and behaves like soft tissue. On a face that lacks skeletal projection it adds width and fullness rather than definition, which is why repeated syringes so often fail to produce the profile the patient wanted. An implant replaces missing structure with structure.

Will there be a scar on my face?

In most cases no. Cheek and chin implants are usually placed through an incision inside the mouth, which leaves nothing visible externally. Where an external approach under the chin is the better choice for a particular anatomy, that is discussed and agreed beforehand.

Do implants wear out or need replacing?

No. They are inert, they do not dissolve, and they do not have a service life. They are replaced only if you want a different result or, rarely, if there is a problem such as infection, which is uncommon and is discussed as part of consent.

What happens at the consultation?

You and Dr. Stone go through your concerns and your goals, she takes a medical history and assesses your general health, and photographs and measurements are taken so the plan is drawn against your own anatomy. You leave knowing whether this is the right procedure for you, and if it is not, which one is.

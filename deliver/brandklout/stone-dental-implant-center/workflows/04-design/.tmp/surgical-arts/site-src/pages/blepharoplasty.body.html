01 · What it removes

Three tissues, and the skin is only the first of them.

The practice describes the upper lid operation in a single sentence: excess skin, muscle and fat, removed and repositioned. Most people arrive believing it is the first of those three, and the part that actually changes how an eye reads is usually the last.

Surface Skin Muscle Fat Deepest

Orbital fat

The fat that sits behind the muscle and pushes forward as the tissue holding it back gives way. It is what a bag under the eye is made of, and it is why bags are there the moment you wake rather than only after a bad night.

Modern technique is increasingly about repositioning this fat into the hollow beneath the eye rather than removing it. Taking it out is how a lid ends up looking hollow instead of rested.

The muscle of the lid

A strip of the muscle that circles the eye is addressed where it is contributing to the fold, at the same time and through the same opening as the skin above it.

This is the layer nobody asks about and the reason two lids that had the same amount of skin taken off can settle differently.

Excess lid skin

The fold that has come to sit on the crease, or on the lashes, or in marked cases into the outer field of vision. It is the layer everybody names first and the easiest of the three to judge in a mirror.

It is also the layer that punishes over-resection hardest on the lower lid. Take too much and the lid pulls away from the eye, which is difficult to correct and immediately obvious.

02 · Who it suits

The test is what happens when the brow is held where it used to sit.

Lift the skin at the tail of your brow with a fingertip. If that single millimetre opens the eye and softens the expression, your operation is a brow lift and not this one. If the eye stays heavy with the brow held up, the weight is in the lid itself, and that is what this page is about.

03 · How it is done

In the crease above, and beneath the lash line below.

On the upper lid the incision is made where the skin naturally folds, so the scar sits in the crease and disappears into it once healed. Excess skin, muscle and fat are removed or repositioned through that one opening.

On the lower lid the incision goes just beneath the lash line, or inside the lid entirely where only fat is being addressed, in which case there is no external scar at all. Which of the two is used depends on whether skin needs removing or only fat needs moving.

It is an outpatient operation and it typically takes an hour or less. Local anaesthetic is used, with sedation or a general where the work or the patient calls for it. The practice uses a long-acting local that can carry pain relief for forty to seventy two hours afterwards, which is why most patients need nothing stronger at home, and you can choose to have a platelet rich plasma or platelet rich fibrin treatment during the procedure to help the site heal.

04 · Recovery

A morning, a week of bruising, and a lid that keeps settling.

This is the shortest recovery of any operation on the practice's surgical list. The figures below are its conventional ranges and they are what to plan around; Dr. Stone will give you the numbers for your own lids at the consultation.

  1. The day

    An hour or less, and home the same day

    One to two hours if both upper and lower lids are being done together. You go home the same day, and somebody else drives: sedation rules that out for you for twenty four hours whatever you feel like.

  2. Days two and three

    Bruising peaks, then falls away

    Bruising is the main event and it is worst around days two and three. Cold compresses and sleeping propped up shorten it considerably. Expect tenderness, watering, light sensitivity and some blurring from the ointment, all of which are on the practice's own list of what is normal.

  3. Week one

    The fine sutures come out

    At around a week. Most of the healing happens inside the first fortnight and most people are back to normal activities inside it, which is what makes this the operation people fit around a job rather than the other way round.

  4. Ten days to three months

    Comfortable in company, then in photographs

    Most people are comfortable in company at ten days and in photographs at three weeks. The last of the swelling in the lid resolves over two to three months, and the scar in the crease keeps fading well past that.

07 · Questions

The six questions this operation raises.

Will the scars show?

On the upper lid the incision sits in the natural crease and disappears into it once healed. On the lower lid it is placed just beneath the lash line, or inside the lid entirely where only fat is being addressed, in which case there is no external scar at all.

How long does it last?

The practice puts eyelid surgery at ten to fifteen years or more, and upper lid surgery is a once in a lifetime operation for most patients. You continue to age, but the tissue that was removed does not come back. Some people choose to repeat it later for sagging that has occurred since.

Will my insurance cover it?

Occasionally, for upper lids only, and only where the excess skin is documented to obstruct the visual field. That requires formal visual field testing and photographs. The practice can tell you whether it is worth pursuing, but it is a question for your insurer and most cases are cosmetic.

Can filler or laser do this instead?

They address different things. Filler can soften a hollow under the eye and laser can improve the texture of thin lid skin, and for early changes either may be enough. Neither removes excess skin or repositions fat that has come forward, which is what makes a lid look heavy.

What happens at the consultation?

You and Dr. Stone go through your concerns and your goals, she takes a medical history and assesses your general health, and photographs and measurements are taken so the plan is drawn against your own anatomy. You leave knowing whether this is the right procedure for you, and if it is not, which one is.

A Prosthodontist's Guide for Peoria PatientsIf you’ve been told you need a dental implant, you’ve probably heard the term “implant crown” — but what exactly does that mean, and how is it different from other types of crowns? At Institute for Advanced Dentistry, we want every patient to walk into their first appointment with a clear picture of what to expect. Here’s what you need to know.
The Three Parts of a Dental Implant
A dental implant isn’t a single piece — it’s a system made up of three components working together:

The implant body — a small titanium (or zirconia) post placed into the jawbone, which acts as an artificial tooth root.
The abutment — a connector piece that attaches to the top of the implant body and creates a stable base for the crown.
The implant crown — the visible, tooth-shaped restoration that sits on top of the abutment and functions like a natural tooth.

The implant crown is the part you see when you smile. It’s custom-made to match the shape, size, and color of your surrounding teeth.
How Is an Implant Crown Different From a Regular Crown?
A traditional dental crown sits on top of a natural tooth that has been reduced in size to accommodate it. An implant crown, by contrast, attaches to an artificial root — there’s no natural tooth structure beneath it.
This distinction matters for a few reasons:

No tooth reduction required — neighboring teeth are left untouched.
Independent restoration — the implant crown doesn’t rely on adjacent teeth for support the way a dental bridge does.
Different seating process — implant crowns are either cemented onto the abutment or secured with a small screw, depending on the case.

Because the crown must integrate precisely with an engineered titanium or zirconia post, the level of precision required is higher than with a standard crown. This is one reason prosthodontists — specialists in tooth replacement and restoration — are uniquely trained to oversee implant-supported restorations.
What Materials Are Used for Implant Crowns?
At Institute for Advanced Dentistry, we follow a porcelain-only philosophy. That means every crown we place is made from zirconia or eMax ceramic — no metal, no porcelain-fused-to-metal (PFM) restorations.
Zirconia is our most commonly recommended material for implant crowns. It’s exceptionally strong, biocompatible, and produces a natural-looking result that holds up well under biting forces — particularly in the back of the mouth where pressure is greatest.
eMax (lithium disilicate) is a glass-ceramic we use in cases where maximum translucency is a priority, typically for front teeth where the aesthetic demands are highest.
For patients interested in a fully metal-free approach, we also offer zirconia implant bodies as an alternative to titanium — a meaningful option for patients with metal sensitivities or those who prefer biocompatible materials throughout their restoration.
What to Expect During the Implant Crown Process
The implant crown is placed after the implant body has fully integrated with the jawbone — a process called osseointegration that typically takes three to six months. During that healing period, we provide a temporary restoration so you’re never without a tooth.
Once integration is confirmed, the process of placing your final implant crown generally involves:

Impressions or digital scans — we capture precise measurements of the implant site and surrounding teeth so the crown fits perfectly.
Crown fabrication — your restoration is sent to a specialist lab and custom-fabricated to match your bite and smile.
Placement and adjustment — the crown is seated, checked for fit and bite, and secured. We make any final adjustments chairside.

At our Peoria office, we use in-house 3D printing for provisional restorations, which means faster turnaround and a more accurate fit during the healing phase.
How Long Does an Implant Crown Last?
With proper care, implant crowns can last 15 to 25 years or longer. The implant body itself, once fully integrated, is designed to be permanent.
Longevity depends on several factors:

Oral hygiene — daily brushing and flossing around the implant site prevents peri-implant disease, which is the leading cause of implant failure.
Material quality — high-grade zirconia and eMax restorations wear more predictably than lower-grade alternatives.
Bite forces — patients who grind or clench their teeth (bruxism) may need a nightguard to protect the crown.
Regular professional maintenance — routine hygiene visits allow us to monitor the implant, abutment, and crown over time.

Why Choose a Prosthodontist for Your Implant Crown?
A prosthodontist is a dental specialist with three additional years of post-doctoral training focused exclusively on the restoration and replacement of teeth. Where a general dentist receives broad training across all areas of dentistry, a prosthodontist’s entire specialty is built around cases like yours — missing teeth, implant restorations, complex bite reconstruction.

Frequently Asked Questions
Is getting an implant crown painful?
The crown placement appointment itself is straightforward and typically requires no anesthesia — you may feel mild pressure, but there’s no drilling involved. Any discomfort during the overall implant process is most commonly associated with the surgical placement of the implant body, not the crown.
How long does an implant crown last?
With good oral hygiene and regular dental care, implant crowns typically last 15 to 25 years. The underlying implant body is designed to last a lifetime when properly maintained.
Can an implant crown be replaced?
Yes. If a crown chips, wears, or needs to be replaced for any reason, the abutment and implant body often remain intact. In most cases, a new crown can be fabricated and seated without disturbing the implant itself.
What’s the difference between an implant crown and a regular crown?
A regular crown restores a damaged natural tooth by capping it. An implant crown replaces a missing tooth entirely by attaching to an artificial root implanted in the jawbone. The two look similar from the outside, but the underlying structure — and the precision required to place them — is quite different.

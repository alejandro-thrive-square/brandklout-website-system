A patient losing or missing most of their teeth has a few restorative options: traditional dentures, snap-in implant dentures, treatments to salvage the failing teeth, or a fixed set of replacement teeth supported by dental implants. The fixed option stays in the mouth and does not rest on the gums for retention. Because it is anchored in bone, it is a surgical procedure as well as a restorative one.

Implant treatment evaluates the bone and the bite. The first weighs available volume, nerve position, sinus proximity, and the depth and angulation the site will actually hold. The second governs occlusal scheme, emergence profile and how the finished tooth loads. The implant has to land in a position that satisfies both.

In some cases, these evaluations are made by clinicians in separate offices. This can impact patient experience and care, especially in more involved cases like full-mouth rehabilitation or restoration of an unsuccessful implant.

“A complex case is planned in reverse. We start from the finished bite and work back to the implant position,” said Dr. Jonathan Bensoussan, a Surgical Prosthodontist at Institute for Advanced Dentistry & Implants and a Phoenix Magazine Top Dentist in Prosthodontics. “The patient experience is more seamless when the same clinician is responsible for the entire scope of work.”

The Two Phases of Implant Treatment, and Why They Belong to One Plan

Implant treatment has a surgical phase and a restorative phase, and dental training has historically separated them into distinct disciplines that follow a referral model. One places the implant and waits for the bone to heal around it. The other builds the teeth: a crown for a single tooth, or a full arch of replacement teeth, commonly known as All-on-4 or 3-on-6.

Information tends to get lost during the handoff. Implant position is a restorative decision made during surgery. Angle it for the bone alone and the crown that follows might have to sit in a location that is hard to clean or loads unevenly. There may not be an issue at first, but the consequences can surface months later.

A solution for this is one clinician carrying both phases. A prosthodontist trained in surgical implant placement plans the restoration and places the implant that will support it, which keeps position and prosthesis inside a single plan.

What to Ask Before Implant Treatment Begins

For patients choosing between dentists, asking the following questions during the consultation can outline expectations around planning, treatment, and recovery.

  1. Who places the implant, and who makes the teeth? Ideally, the same dentist will place the implant and make the teeth, providing a more seamless experience for patients. If there are two dentists involved, ask to meet with the one who designed the plan.
  2. What imaging is taken before the plan is written? A CBCT 3D scan shows bone volume, nerve position and sinus anatomy that a standard dental X-ray does not. This makes the procedure’s outcomes more predictable.
  3. Is the final result designed before surgery? Digital scanning and smile design let the restorative outcome drive implant position.
  4. What does maintenance look like? Ask about recovery and maintenance, as well as follow-up visits. It’s also worth noting the process for future adjustments.
  5. What would make me a poor candidate? Appropriate candidates are identified through examination.

What Happens When Implant Work Has Failed?

Failed or unsatisfactory implants, crowns, bridges, veneers and previous full-arch treatment form a category of their own, and the correction starts with a diagnosis. The next step is to establish why the first treatment failed and what the bone looks like now. Bone changes can occur after an implant is lost, so managing dental implant complications often involves bone grafting and a revised position. A loose restoration or a failing implant keeps loading bone that is already compromised, and the correction becomes more complex the longer that continues.

“Many patients assume a failed implant gets swapped out with a new one, but the same spot that failed once has a 30% chance of failure again if we don’t make changes,” Dr. Bensoussan said. “That’s why timing is important. The longer a bad implant sits, the more bone a patient loses, and the fewer good positions we have to work with.”

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I see a general dentist or a specialist for dental implants in Peoria?

Both can place implants in Arizona. The difference comes down to training. A prosthodontist completes three to four additional years of residency focused on replacing and restoring teeth, the phase of the process that decides how the finished implant looks and functions. This level of education matters most for complex cases, such as a full-arch treatment or a failed implant.

What happens if a dental implant fails?

The site is assessed before replacement. A CBCT 3D scan shows how much bone remains and whether infection, position or loading caused the failure. Treatment can involve removing the implant, grafting the site, allowing it to heal, and placing a new implant in a revised position.

Can the same dentist place the implant and make the final teeth?

Yes. A prosthodontist trained in surgical implant placement can manage both phases, which keeps the implant position and the finished restoration inside one plan. At Institute for Advanced Dentistry & Implants, the same clinician plans the case, performs the surgery and delivers the finished teeth.

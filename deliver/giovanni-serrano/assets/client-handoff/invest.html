Buying your first multifamily? Model the deal first.

Most first-time buyers walk into a property and try to imagine themselves living there. The right approach is the opposite: walk into the numbers first. Run the proforma. Stress the assumptions. Decide whether to fall for the property only after the math says it's worth falling for.

Why Multifamily

A duplex, triplex, or fourplex isn't just a different kind of house. It's a different financial instrument.

Most first-time buyers default to a single-family home because that's what their friends bought. But for the same down payment, the same monthly payment, the same neighborhood — a 2-to-4-unit property does something a single-family home structurally cannot:

01.
Your tenants pay most of your mortgage
On a typical San Diego fourplex, the rent from the three units you don't live in often covers most of the mortgage, taxes, insurance, and reserves. Your effective housing cost ends up below renting the same unit. You're not just buying a place to live. You're buying an asset that pays you to live there.
02.
Equity builds faster — multiple sources, not just your paycheck
A single-family buyer pays down their loan with one source: their own income. A multifamily buyer has three or four sources paying down the loan — three or four tenants, plus you. Same down payment. Substantially more equity built over five years.
03.
Cash flow, not a money pit
A single-family home costs you money every month — mortgage, taxes, insurance, repairs, no offsetting income. A well-priced multifamily produces cash flow once it stabilizes — even after debt service. That difference compounds over decades.
04.
When you sell, you're selling a business — not just a house
A single-family home sells on what other homes nearby sold for. A multifamily property sells on its NOI capitalized at the local cap rate. The income you build over time directly drives what you can sell for — independent of what your neighbors' houses are doing. Two paths, two valuation regimes, two very different exit prices.
The Strategy

Live in one unit. Rent the rest. Buy with owner-occupied financing.

A 2-to-4-unit property bought as your primary residence qualifies for the same low-down-payment loans a single-family buyer gets — FHA at 3.5% down on credit-qualified buyers, or conventional at 5% down. As an investor on the same property, you'd be putting down 25%. The difference is what makes this strategy work for someone buying their first property.

A.
Owner-occupied loan products
FHA loans on 2-to-4-unit owner-occupied properties have historically gone as low as 3.5% down for credit-qualified buyers. Conventional owner-occupied multifamily loans often start around 5% down. The same fourplex an investor would put $400K down on, an owner-occupant might enter for under $90K — and let the tenants cover most of the mortgage.
B.
Rental income to qualify
Most owner-occupied multifamily lenders will count a portion of the projected rents from the non-owner units toward your debt-to-income calculation when you qualify. That means the property's rents help you qualify for the property — your personal income doesn't have to do all the work.
C.
An asset that produces income
After your owner-occupancy period (typically one year for FHA), you can move out and put your unit on the rental market. Now you own an income-producing investment property — and you can repeat the strategy on the next purchase.
D.
Tax treatment a single-family buyer doesn't get
The rental portion of the property is depreciable. Operating expenses on the rental units (repairs, insurance, a share of property taxes, mortgage interest) are deductible against rental income. Single-family ownership has none of this. Talk to your CPA on specifics — but structurally, multifamily ownership is more tax-efficient.

How I Model Your Deal

The proforma — line by line, the way real estate finance defines it.

Most agents will tell you a property "should cash flow." That isn't analysis. The standard income-property proforma — the one every lender, appraiser, and institutional investor uses — works through the same line items in the same order. Here's exactly what I run for you:

1
Gross scheduled rent (potential gross income)
Total rent the property would produce at 100% occupancy and current market rents. Built up unit-by-unit using the actual rent roll for occupied units and verified rent comps from the surrounding submarket for any units below market or vacant. If the seller's listing claims rents that don't match the rent roll, we catch that here.
2
Less vacancy & collection loss → effective gross income (EGI)
Even fully-leased properties model 5–10% vacancy. We lose units to turnover, late rent, and the occasional bad tenant. Pulling realistic vacancy out gives us effective gross income — the rent the property is actually likely to collect.
3
Less operating expenses → net operating income (NOI)
Property taxes, insurance, utilities the owner pays, management (real or imputed at 7–10%), maintenance, reserves for replacements, common-area items. The standard "operating expense ratio" for small multifamily typically runs in the 35–45% range of EGI. NOI is the single most important number on the page — it's what cap rates are computed against, what lenders underwrite against, and what the property's value as an investment ultimately rests on.
4
Less debt service → before-tax cash flow (BTCF)
Annual principal-and-interest payments on your mortgage, based on your specific loan amount, rate, and amortization. NOI minus debt service equals your cash flow — what actually shows up in your account each year before taxes. (In real estate finance, this is also called the "equity dividend" — and it's the foundation of your return on equity.)
5
Compute the four metrics that actually matter
From those line items, the proforma generates the metrics that lenders, appraisers, and investors all use to evaluate income property: cap rate (NOI ÷ purchase price — the unleveraged yield), cash-on-cash return (BTCF ÷ cash invested — your actual yield on equity), debt service coverage ratio (NOI ÷ debt service — lenders want at least 1.20), and break-even occupancy (the occupancy rate at which the property covers debt + operating expenses but nothing more).
6
Stress-test on the variables that actually move
What if vacancy is 10% instead of 5%? What if rates rise half a point at your refinance? What if one unit takes 60 days to turn? The deal is the deal that survives the downside case — not the one that only works at perfect assumptions. Sensitivity analysis is the difference between an underwrite and a sales pitch.

Reading the metrics
Cap rate of 5.4% ($88,920 NOI ÷ $1,650,000) is the unleveraged yield — what the property earns regardless of how it's financed. Cash-on-cash of −2.1% ($−9,852 BTCF ÷ $462,500 cash invested) is the leveraged yield to a 25%-down investor — slightly negative in this rate environment. DSCR of 0.90 (NOI ÷ debt service) means NOI doesn't fully cover the mortgage payment — most lenders want at least 1.20, so as a pure investor deal at these terms, this property would have trouble qualifying for the loan as-is. But this changes dramatically as an owner-occupant. If you live in one of the four units, you reduce gross rent by one unit (−$36K/yr) but you also eliminate your separate housing cost (a comparable 2BR rental in North Park runs ~$2,800/mo = ~$33,600/yr). Net to you on the math: roughly housing-cost-neutral, while 25% of the property accrues equity in your name and the other 75% gets paid down by your tenants. Run the same property at 5% down owner-occupied financing instead of 25% investor down, and the total cash to close drops from $462K to closer to $115K — and the math gets meaningfully better.

Common Questions

What first-time buyers usually ask before we get started.

Do I need a big down payment?
Not necessarily. As an owner-occupant on a 2-to-4-unit, FHA financing has historically gone as low as 3.5% down for credit-qualified buyers, and conventional owner-occupied multifamily loans often start around 5% down. As a non-owner-occupant investor on the same property, you'd typically need 25% down. We'll go through the loan products you actually qualify for in the first session — exact down-payment requirements vary by program, lender, and your specific credit profile.
Will I have to be a landlord and deal with tenants?
In a 2-to-4-unit you typically inherit existing tenants and existing leases at close. Day-to-day landlording is mostly low-frequency: rent collection, occasional repair coordination, lease renewals. Many first-time owners self-manage. Some hire a small property manager for 7–10% of gross rent. Either is workable. We'll talk through both before you decide.
What if rents drop or I have a bad tenant?
That's exactly what stress-testing the proforma is for. Before we make an offer I model what happens at higher vacancy, what happens with one tenant turnover, what happens if a unit needs a 60-day rent-ready turn. The deal we move forward with should still pencil under realistic downside scenarios — not just under perfect conditions. The "break-even occupancy rate" tells you exactly how empty the property can get before it stops covering its own bills.
How is working with you different from a typical agent?
Most agents are showing properties and writing offers. I'm doing those things too, but my main role is treating your purchase like an investment — running the proforma, stress-testing the numbers, and helping you understand what the asset actually does. By the time you close, you should be able to explain the four key metrics on your own deal: cap rate, cash-on-cash return, DSCR, and break-even occupancy.
What does it cost to work with you?
